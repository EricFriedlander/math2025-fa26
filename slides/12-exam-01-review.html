Models and EDA (Lectures 0-3)

You should be able to…

  • Identify the response (outcome, target) and explanatory (predictor, input) variables in a research question.
  • Say whether a variable is quantitative or categorical, and pick a sensible plot:
Setting Plot
One quantitative Histogram, boxplot, jitter plot
One categorical Bar chart
Quant vs. quant Scatterplot
Quant vs. categorical Side-by-side boxplots or jitter plots
  • Tell univariate, bivariate, and multivariate analysis apart.
  • Describe a scatterplot: direction, form, strength, unusual points.
  • Explain why we fit a model: prediction vs. inference, and give an example of each.

Statistical model vs. regression equation

Statistical model (population, has error)

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon\]

  • Describes how the response is generated
  • Greek letters: unknown parameters

Regression equation (sample, no error)

\[\hat{Y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 X\]

  • Our estimate of the function
  • Hats: statistics computed from data
  • Be able to say which one a given formula is, and why the second has no \(\epsilon\).
  • “All models are wrong, but some are useful” (Box). A model is a simplification.

Simpson’s paradox and confounding

  • Simpson’s paradox: a relationship between two variables disappears or reverses once you account for a third variable.
  • A confounder is related to both the explanatory and the response variable.
  • Be able to explain, in words, why the pooled trend and the within-group trends can disagree.
  • Be able to name a plausible confounder in a new context.

Reading dplyr code

You will not write code, but you should be able to say what a short pipeline does or what the result looks like.

Verb What it does
filter(x == "a") Keeps rows where the condition is true
mutate(new = ...) Creates or changes columns
group_by(g) then summarize(m = mean(y)) One summary row per group
  • |> passes the result on the left into the next function.
  • == tests equality, != means “not equal,” and strings go in quotes.
  • A factor stores categories as ordered levels. The first level is R’s default reference level.

Simple linear regression and categorical predictors (Lectures 4-5)

Interpreting a slope and an intercept

For \(\widehat{\text{count}} = 2077.5 + 152.9 \times \text{temp}\) (this is the lecture example):

  • Slope: for each 1-unit increase in the predictor, the response is predicted to change by \(\hat{\beta}_1\) units, on average. Include the units and the context.
  • Intercept: the predicted response when the predictor is 0.
    • Is 0 inside the range of the data? If not, the intercept is extrapolation and may be meaningless.
  • Prediction: plug in \(x\) and compute \(\hat{y}\).
  • Extrapolation: predicting far outside the range of the data is risky.

Warning

Use words like “predicted” and “on average.” A slope describes an association, not a cause, unless the data came from a randomized experiment.

Categorical predictors

  • A \(K\)-level categorical variable becomes \(K - 1\) indicator (dummy) variables.
  • The left-out level is the reference level (baseline). R picks it alphabetically unless the variable is a factor with set levels.
  • The intercept is the mean of the response at the reference level.
  • Each coefficient is the difference in means between that level and the reference level.

Given a table of coefficients you should be able to:

  • Identify the reference level (it is the level with no row in the table).
  • Compute the predicted mean for every level.
  • Explain why correlation does not apply to a categorical predictor even though regression does.

Residuals, least squares, and model evaluation (Lectures 6-7)

Residuals and least squares

\[e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i \qquad \text{(observed } - \text{ predicted)}\]

  • Positive residual: the point is above the line (the model underpredicted).
  • Negative residual: the point is below the line (the model overpredicted).
  • Sum of squared errors: \(SSE = \sum e_i^2\).
  • Least squares picks \(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1\) to make \(SSE\) as small as possible.
  • Why square? So positive and negative residuals don’t cancel.
  • The mean is the single number that minimizes \(SSE\). The empty model predicts \(\bar{y}\) for everyone.

Properties of least squares

  • The line passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\), so \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\).
  • \(\hat{\beta}_1 = r \dfrac{s_y}{s_x}\), so the slope has the same sign as the correlation \(r\).
  • The residuals sum to 0.
  • The residuals are uncorrelated with \(x\).

Tip

You can go from a slope and \(\bar{x}, \bar{y}\) to an intercept, or from \(r, s_x, s_y\) to a slope, with a calculator. Practice both directions.

Partitioning variation (ANOVA)

\[\underbrace{SST}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{SSM}_{\text{explained by model}} + \underbrace{SSE}_{\text{left over}}\]

  • \(SST = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\) is the \(SSE\) of the empty model.
  • \(SSM = SST - SSE\).
  • \(SSE\) cannot be bigger than \(SST\), so \(SSM \geq 0\).

\[R^2 = PRE = \frac{SSM}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}\]

  • Between 0 and 1, no units.
  • Interpretation: “\(R^2 \times 100\)% of the variability in [response] is explained by [predictor].”
  • In simple linear regression, \(R^2 = r^2\) (so you can recover \(|r|\), but get the sign from the slope).

RMSE

\[RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n}}\]

  • Roughly: how far the model misses by, on average.
  • Between 0 and \(\infty\), in the same units as the response.
  • Smaller is better, but “good” depends on context.
  • \(R^2\) and RMSE answer different questions: proportion explained vs. typical miss.

You should be able to:

  • Fill in a missing entry of an ANOVA table given the other two.
  • Compare two models using \(R^2\) and RMSE, and explain why the one with more flexibility (e.g., a categorical price vs. a numeric price) can never have a lower \(R^2\).
  • Explain why you can’t say a model is “good” from \(R^2\) alone.

Study design and sampling (Lectures 8-9)

Population, sample, parameter, statistic

Population Sample
Mean \(\mu\) \(\bar{x}\)
Slope \(\beta_1\) \(\hat{\beta}_1\) (or \(\hat{\beta}_1\))
  • A parameter describes the population. A statistic describes the sample.
  • Statistical inference: using a statistic to say something about a parameter.
  • Two threats: a bad sample (bias) and sampling variability (chance).

Studies and conclusions

Random assignment No random assignment
Random sample Causal, and generalizes to the population Association only, but generalizes
No random sample Causal, but only for the units in the study Association only, only for the units in the study
  • Observational study: researchers observe. Experiment: researchers assign.
  • Random assignment supports causal claims. Random sampling supports generalizing.
  • Given a study description, be able to place it in this table and justify it.

Sampling methods and bias

Method How it works Goal
Simple random sample Every unit equally likely Representative
Stratified Groups differ from each other, sample from every group Make sure every kind of unit is represented
Cluster Groups look alike, sample some groups Save time and money
Convenience Whoever is easy to reach (Not good)
  • Undercoverage: part of the population isn’t in the sampling frame.
  • Non-response bias, survivorship bias, and convenience bias: be able to recognize each.
  • Bias comes from the process, not the sample size. A bigger biased sample is still biased.

Shuffling and p-values (Lecture 10)

The logic of a shuffling test

  1. Hypotheses: \(H_0\) says there is no relationship. \(H_A\) says there is one.
  2. Pick a test statistic (PRE, \(\hat{\beta}_1\), …).
  3. Shuffle the response to break any relationship, and recompute the statistic. Repeat many times.
  4. The histogram of shuffled statistics is the null distribution: what we’d see by chance if \(H_0\) were true.
  5. The p-value is the proportion of shuffles at least as extreme as our real statistic.
  • Shuffling keeps the set of values and the group sizes. It breaks the pairing between \(x\) and \(y\).
  • PRE is never negative, so even a useless predictor has \(PRE > 0\). That is why we need a null distribution.
  • Shuffling accounts for chance. It does not fix bias or tell us about cause.

Interpreting a p-value

The p-value is the probability of getting a statistic at least as extreme as ours, if the null hypothesis is true.

p-value Evidence against \(H_0\)
\(< 0.01\) strong
\(0.01\) to \(0.05\) moderate
\(0.05\) to \(0.1\) weak
\(> 0.1\) effectively none

Important

Common wrong answers. The p-value is not:

  • the probability that \(H_0\) is true,
  • the probability that the results happened “by chance,”
  • a measure of how big or important the effect is.

A large p-value means “no convincing evidence,” not “evidence of no relationship.”

Inference for the slope (Lecture 11)

Hypothesis test for the slope

\[H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \qquad H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\]

  • The null distribution of \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is normal, centered at 0, with spread \(SE_{\hat{\beta}_1}\).
  • \(SE_{\hat{\beta}_1}\) is the standard error: the SD of \(\hat{\beta}_1\) across samples.

\[t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1 - 0}{SE_{\hat{\beta}_1}} = \frac{\text{estimate} - \text{null value}}{\text{standard error}}\]

  • \(t\) counts “how many standard errors is our slope from the null value?”
  • The p-value comes from a \(t\) distribution with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom. It is two-sided when \(H_A\) has \(\neq\).
  • Why \(n-2\)? We estimated two parameters, \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\).
  • The \(t\) has heavier tails than the normal, which matters for small \(n\).

What makes \(SE_{\hat{\beta}_1}\) small?

\[SE_{\hat{\beta}_1} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_\epsilon}{\sqrt{(n-1)s_X^2}}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}_\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n-2}}\]

  • Larger \(n\): smaller \(SE\).
  • More spread in \(x\): smaller \(SE\).
  • Less scatter around the line: smaller \(SE\).

You should be able to say what happens to the \(SE\), the \(t\) statistic, and the p-value when one of these changes, holding the others fixed.

The normal curve

  • A normal distribution has a center \(\mu\) and spread \(\sigma\).
  • About 68% of values are within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD, and 99.7% within 3 SD.

You should be able to use these without a computer:

  • “A statistic is 2 SD from the center. Roughly what share of values are more extreme?” About 5% in two tails.
  • “Is a \(t\) of 0.4 or a \(t\) of 4 more surprising under the null?”

Reading R output

A fake example, start to finish

A study of \(n = 25\) days relates ice cream sales (in hundreds of dollars) to temperature (in degrees C). Here is the output:

term estimate std.error statistic p.value
(Intercept) 12.0 6.1 1.97 0.061
temp 3.5 1.4 2.50 0.020
term df sumsq
Model 1 400
Error 23 600
Total 24 1000

Reading it

  • Equation: \(\widehat{\text{sales}} = 12.0 + 3.5 \times \text{temp}\)
  • Prediction at 20 degrees: \(12.0 + 3.5(20) = 82\), or $8,200.
  • Slope: each extra degree is associated with 3.5 more hundred dollars in sales, on average.
  • \(t\): \(3.5 / 1.4 = 2.5\) (each row’s statistic is estimate / std.error).
  • p = 0.020: moderate evidence of a linear relationship.
  • \(R^2\) \(= 400/1000 = 0.40\): 40% of the variability in sales is explained by temperature.
  • RMSE \(= \sqrt{600/25} = \sqrt{24} \approx 4.9\) hundred dollars.
  • \(\hat{\sigma}_\epsilon = \sqrt{600/23} \approx 5.1\).

Also notice: degrees of freedom for the error row are \(n - 2 = 23\), and the total is \(n - 1 = 24\).

Other output you might see

  • A residual plot or a scatterplot with a fitted line: be able to say whether points above/below the line have positive/negative residuals.
  • A histogram of shuffled statistics with a red line for the observed value: be able to say roughly what the p-value is (the share of the histogram at least as extreme as the line).
  • dplyr code with a small data table: be able to say what rows and columns remain.
  • A table of group means: be able to turn it into a regression equation with a reference level.
