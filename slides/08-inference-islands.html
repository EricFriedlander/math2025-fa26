Population and sample

  • Population: the complete set of observations we care about
  • Sample: the subset we actually observe (denote sample size \(n\))
  • A parameter is a number describing the population: \(\mu\), \(\beta_1\)
  • A statistic is a number computed from the sample: \(\bar{x}\), \(\hat{\beta}_1\)
Population Sample
Size \(N\) (sometimes) \(n\)
Mean \(\mu\) \(\bar{x}\)
Slope \(\beta_1\) \(\hat{\beta}_1\)

We (almost) never get to see the parameter.

We see a statistic and use that to reason about the parameter.

Sampling is natural

  • When you taste a spoonful of soup and decide it isn’t salty enough, that’s exploratory analysis.
  • If you conclude that the entire pot needs salt, that’s an inference.
  • For your inference to be valid, the spoonful (the sample) needs to be representative of the pot (the population).

Statistical inference

Statistical inference refers to ideas, methods, and tools for generalizing from the single sample we observed to statements about the population it came from.

Two things to account for:

  1. A bad sample. If the sample doesn’t represent the population, we are biased, and no amount of math can fix that. This is today.
  2. Sampling variability. Even a perfectly good sample gives a slightly different answer each time. We can quantify this. This is the next few weeks.

Confounding and Simpson’s Paradox

Controlling for species

What does the relationship look like now?

Simpson’s Paradox

Model Slope for bill length
bill_depth_mm ~ bill_length_mm -0.09
bill_depth_mm ~ bill_length_mm + species 0.2

Simpson’s Paradox: a relationship between two variables disappears or reverses once you account for a third variable.

  • Species is a confounding variable: it is related to both bill length (Gentoos have long bills) and bill depth (Gentoos have shallow bills).
  • Pooling the species makes “longer bill” secretly mean “more likely a Gentoo.”
  • Within any one species, longer bills go with deeper bills.

What is a confounder?

A confounding variable is related to both the explanatory and the response variable, so it can create, hide, or even reverse an apparent relationship.

  • The pooled model and the species model use the same data. Which slope is “right”?
  • Why is this a bigger worry in an observational study than in an experiment?
  • Could there be a confounder we didn’t measure?
  • Random assignment breaks the link between the explanatory variable and every confounder, measured or not.
  • Basic idea: by randomly assigning the explanatory variable, the confounding variables should be kind of balanced

Statistical Inference/Generalization

Another question:

  • Do the results we get from these penguins apply to all penguins?
  • More generally: do the patterns we see in our sample apply to our population?

Simple Random Sample

  • For the answer to these questions to be yes… we must have a representative sample
  • Intuitively: our sample should look like a mini-version of our population
  • Simple random sample: every member of the population is equally likely to be chosen
  • This random sampling allows us to generalize back to the population
  • Idea: by randomly choosing observations, we’re not going to accidentally miss any patterns

Sampling is hard !

  • Reality: usually impossible or impractical to do a truly SRS
  • You will usually need to argue why your sample is “close enough” to a SRS or is a representative sample using the principles we discuss here

Let’s go through some additional sampling techniques that either improve your sample or simplify your sampling in a principled way

Stratification

Suppose you want to conduct a political poll to find out whether a certain ballot measure will pass in the upcoming election:

  • You know that the party affiliation breakdown in Idaho is about 63% Republican, 25% Independent, 12% Democrat and you know that a persons party affiliation will probably explain a lot of the variation in which why they decide to vote on the ballot measure
  • If you collect a simple random sample, you may get something like 60% R, 23% I, 17% D
  • Stratification/Stratified sampling: split the population into groups (strata) of similar individuals, then randomly sample from every stratum.
  • To get a more accurate poll you might employ stratified sampling by party affiliation
  • Real polls stratify by many different attributes (race, gender, party affiliation, educational attainment, income, etc…)

Cluster Sampling

Suppose it is the summer of 1985 and the very first test for HIV has become available. You would like to use it to estimate the prevalence of HIV in the nations blood supply. There are over 1,000 blood banks in the US and it is prohibitively expensive to collect samples from all of them… the internet doesn’t exist and you don’t have the manpower to sent out letters to thousands of blood banks and keep track of the replies.

  • Instead you randomly select 30 different blood banks in the US
  • Test all of the donations in each blood bank
  • Cluster sample: split the population into groups (clusters) that each look like a mini-population, randomly choose some clusters, and sample within those.

Strata vs. clusters

Stratified

  • Groups are different from each other
  • Sample from every group
  • Goal: make sure every kind of unit is represented

Cluster

  • Groups are similar to each other
  • Sample from some groups
  • Goal: save time and money

Real Samples Combine These Techniques

  • Most sampling techniques combine elements of Simple Random Sampling, Stratified Sampling, and Cluster Sampling
    • It is called Multistage Sampling if you first do, say, cluster sampling (stage 1) and then stratified sampling inside each cluster (stage 2)
  • Other (not very good) forms of sampling:
    • Convenience sample: whoever is easiest to reach. Watch out.
    • Judgement sample: using your own personal judgement to decide who to same. Easily introduce unconscious bias.

How a sample goes wrong

  • Convenience bias: you sample the units that are easy to get.
  • Undercoverage: part of the population isn’t in your sampling frame (the list you sample from) at all.
  • Selection bias: the way units enter the data is related to the outcome. Many types of selection bias:
  • Non-response bias: the people you chose don’t respond, and they differ from the ones who do.
  • Survivorship bias: your sample consists solely of observations that have successfully made it through some process
  • Bias is a property of the sampling process, not the sample size
  • Reality: impossible to design a perfect experiment to study… you must argue why these types of bias aren’t an issue

Welcome to the Islands

The Islands

A simulated world of thousands of islanders, organized into villages across several islands.

  • You can look up islanders, interview them, and measure things about them.
  • You can assign islanders to treatments, which means you can run experiments as well as observational studies.

  • It’s a simulation, so, in principle, we could census the whole population and check whether our inference was right.

Life expectancy is trickier than it sounds

Life expectancy is the average number of years a newborn today could expect to live, if current death rates stayed the same.

The previous class computed it properly with age-specific mortality rates (a life table), which is what demographers do. It’s a lot of tabulating.

Today, we’ll use a simpler stand-in: the mean age at death among islanders who have died.

  • What’s the danger in averaging age at death among only the people who have died recently?
  • Who is missing from that group?

Someone born last year can’t appear in it. Neither can anyone who hasn’t died yet. Keep this in mind, since we’ll come back to it.

Once we compiled the data for approximately 500 people, we selected the people who died between 1980 and 2010 as well as those who are alive today. We made the assumption that this selection would give us a representative picture of present or contemporary life expectancy, since living conditions throughout the last thirty years could be considered relatively consistent and relevant. We also capped the age at 150, since our oldest person in the sample was 147. Recap

  • Population vs. sample, parameter vs. statistic: inference means using a statistic like \(\hat{\beta}_1\) to learn about a parameter like \(\beta_1\).
  • Observational studies observe; experiments assign. Only random assignment supports causal claims, and only random sampling supports generalizing.
  • Confounders are related to both the explanatory and response variables.
  • Stratified samples take units from every group; cluster samples take all or some units from some groups. Bias comes from the sampling process, not the sample size.
