Part 1: Review of Lectures 0-10

Models, EDA, and dplyr

The full list is in the Exam 1 study guide. The highlights that tend to come back:

  • Response vs. explanatory variable; quantitative vs. categorical; picking a plot (scatterplot, boxplots or jitter plot by group, histogram, bar chart).
  • Statistical model \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon\) (population, Greek letters, has error) vs. regression equation \(\hat{Y} = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X\) (sample, hats, no error).
  • Simpson’s paradox and confounding variables: a third variable related to both the explanatory and the response variable.
  • Reading code: filter() keeps rows, mutate() makes or changes columns, group_by() + summarize() gives one row per group.

SLR, categorical predictors, residuals

  • Slope: for each 1-unit increase in \(x\), the response is predicted to change by \(\hat\beta_1\) units, on average. Intercept: predicted response at \(x = 0\). May not make sense. Check if there are x-values around zero. If not \(\Rightarrow\) interpreting it is extrapolation.
  • Categorical predictor: \(K\) levels give \(K - 1\) indicators. The intercept is the mean at the reference level, and each coefficient is a difference in means from the reference level.
  • Residual \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\). Positive: point above the line (underpredicted). Least squares minimizes \(SSE = \sum e_i^2\). Linear models balance the residuals so they should sum to 0.
  • \(SST = SSM + SSE\), \(\;R^2 = PRE = \dfrac{SSM}{SST} = 1 - \dfrac{SSE}{SST}\), \(\;RMSE = \sqrt{SSE/n}\) (same units as \(y\)).
  • The empty model predicts \(\bar y\) for everyone. \(R^2\) and RMSE answer different questions.

Study design, sampling, shuffling

  • Parameter (population) vs. statistic (sample).
  • Random assignment supports causal claims. Random sampling supports generalizing. Place a study in the 2 × 2 table and justify it.
  • Simple random, stratified (sample from every group), cluster (sample some groups), convenience. Bias comes from the process, not the sample size.
  • Shuffling: break the pairing between \(x\) and \(y\), recompute the statistic, repeat. The histogram is the null distribution. The p-value is the share of shuffles at least as extreme as the real statistic.
  • A p-value is not the probability \(H_0\) is true. A large p-value means “no convincing evidence,” not “evidence of no relationship.”

Part 2: Inference for a slope (Lectures 11, 14-16)

Lecture 11: the \(t\)-test for a slope

Hypotheses: \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) vs. \(H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\) (\(\beta_1\) is the population slope, \(\hat\beta_1\) the sample slope).

\[SE_{\hat\beta_1} = \frac{\hat\sigma_\epsilon}{\sqrt{(n-1)\,s_X^2}}, \qquad \hat\sigma_\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n-2}}, \qquad T = \frac{\hat\beta_1 - 0}{SE_{\hat\beta_1}}\]

  • You will not be expected to do calculations with these formulas, but you are expected to understand how these quantities change directionally. I.e. “If I increase the sample size without changing anything else, what happens to \(SE_{\hat\beta_1}\)?”
  • \(T\) follows a \(t\) distribution with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom (heavier tails than the normal, because \(\sigma\) is estimated).
  • Interpret \(T\): “our slope is \(T\) standard errors above/below what we’d expect if there were no linear relationship.”
  • What makes \(SE\) smaller? Larger \(n\), more spread in \(X\) (larger \(s_X\)), less scatter around the line (smaller \(\hat\sigma_\epsilon\)).
  • The shuffled-slope null distribution is symmetric, centered at 0, and (unlike PRE) can be negative, so the p-value is two-sided. Its standard deviation is close to the std.error in tidy().
  • Conclusion template: “The data provide (strong / moderate / weak / no) evidence that the population slope for [x] and [y] differs from 0, i.e., of a linear relationship.”

Lecture 14: decisions, errors, power

  • Decision rule: choose \(\alpha\) before looking (usually 0.05). If \(p < \alpha\), reject \(H_0\). Otherwise fail to reject \(H_0\). Never “accept” \(H_0\).
  • “Nothing magical happens at \(\alpha\)”: \(p = 0.049\) and \(p = 0.051\) are nearly identical evidence.
\(H_0\) true \(H_0\) false
Reject \(H_0\) Type I error (false positive) correct (this is power)
Fail to reject correct Type II error (false negative)
  • \(P(\text{Type I}) = \alpha\). Power \(= 1 - P(\text{Type II})\).
  • Power goes up with: larger \(n\), larger \(\alpha\), less noise (\(\hat\sigma_\epsilon\) smaller), a larger true slope, more spread in \(X\).
  • Lowering \(\alpha\) trades fewer Type I errors for more Type II errors.
  • Power analysis: we choose \(\alpha\) and \(n\) (or a target power, often 80%), and we must assume a true slope and \(\sigma\). Do it before collecting data. Power computed from the effect you already observed is not a guarantee.

Lectures 15-16: effect size

  • An effect size measures how big a relationship is relative to the noise in the data.
    • Quantitative predictor: \(R^2\) (PRE).
    • Two groups: Cohen’s \(d\), \(d = \dfrac{\hat\beta_1}{\hat\sigma_\epsilon} = \dfrac{\bar y_1 - \bar y_2}{s_{pooled}}\).
  • The noise is the spread of the data, not the standard error. \(SE\) shrinks with \(n\), so it is the wrong yardstick.
  • Detectable is not the same as big. With a huge \(n\), a tiny effect can have a tiny p-value. With a small \(n\), a big effect can have a large p-value.
  • Effect size does not grow with \(n\). P-values shrink with \(n\). Bigger effect size means more power at the same \(n\).
  • Statistical vs. practical significance: compare the estimate (or its interval) to a break-even size that matters in context.
  • \(p = 0.30\) with a small \(n\) does not mean “no effect”: it may be a Type II error (low power).
  • There are no universal cutoffs for “small” or “large”: meaning depends on context.

Lecture 16 & Pilot Project Reading: study design vocabulary

  • If you didn’t, make sure to read this guide to study design from the Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (University of Oxford).
  • Randomized experiment: researchers assign treatments at random. The only design that supports strong causal claims.
  • Observational: descriptive vs. analytical (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional). (These are from assigned reading for your project).
  • Bias types: selection, allocation, performance. Blinding reduces performance and measurement bias.
  • Be able to say which design a description is, and what conclusion (causal or association, which population) it supports.

Part 3: Multiple regression (Lectures 17-23)

Lecture 17: the MLR model

\[\hat Y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 X_1 + \hat\beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \hat\beta_p X_p\]

  • Interpreting a coefficient: “\(\hat\beta_j\) is the expected change in the mean of \(y\) when \(x_j\) increases by one unit, holding the values of all other predictors constant.”
  • A categorical predictor with \(k\) levels gives \(k-1\) indicators. Each indicator coefficient is a difference from the reference level, holding the others constant.
  • Additive model with one quantitative and one categorical predictor = parallel lines: one slope, a different intercept per level. The indicator coefficient is the vertical gap between the lines.
  • Coefficients, SEs, t-statistics, and p-values change when you add predictors, especially if the predictors are related. The SLR slope can be “absorbing” a confounder.
  • Be able to explain why an MLR coefficient differs from the SLR slope for the same variable.

Lecture 18: prediction and interactions

  • Prediction: plug values into the fitted equation. Don’t extrapolate, including “in many directions at once”: each predictor can be in range while the combination is not.
  • Interaction: \(\mu_Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 X_1 X_2\). “The effect of \(X_1\) depends on \(X_2\).” Keep the single-order terms when you keep the interaction.
Case What \(\hat\beta_3\) means
Categorical × categorical Difference in differences: extra effect of \(X_1\) for the non-reference level of \(X_2\)
Categorical × quantitative Change in slope from the reference group: slopes are \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_3\)
Quantitative × quantitative Slope of \(X_1\) is \(\hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_3 X_2\) (changes continuously)
  • With an interaction there is no single effect: describe the effect at a value of the other variable.
  • Without it, “main effects” are effects at the reference level of the other variable.
  • Be able to compute every group’s mean or line from a table of coefficients.

Lecture 19: inference for MLR coefficients

  • \(H_0: \beta_j = 0\) given the other predictors are in the model. \(H_A: \beta_j \neq 0\) given the others.

\[T = \frac{\hat\beta_j - 0}{SE_{\hat\beta_j}}, \quad SE_{\hat\beta_j} = \frac{\hat\sigma_\epsilon}{\sqrt{(n-1)\,s_{X_j}^2\,(1 - R_j^2)}}, \quad \hat\sigma_\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{SSE}{n-p}}\]

  • Again… not expecting you to do calculations with these but you will be expected to know how they change directionally in response to changes in things like sample size, residual error, etc…
  • \(T\) follows a \(t\) distribution with \(n - p\) df, where \(p\) counts all coefficients including the intercept.
  • \(R_j^2\) is the \(R^2\) from predicting \(X_j\) from the other predictors. Overlap among predictors inflates the SE.
  • The p-value answers a different question in each model. Estimates change through confounding. SEs change when \(\hat\sigma_\epsilon\) shrinks or when predictors overlap.
  • In a shuffling test for MLR, shuffle the response, not an explanatory variable (shuffling \(X\) breaks the correlation among predictors, so the null is too narrow).
  • Categorical \(t\)-tests compare each level to the reference level.

Lecture 20: nested models and the F-test

  • Model A is nested in model B if B has every term of A plus more. Call them reduced and full. (Sales ~ Temperature is not nested in Sales ~ Location.)
  • \(H_0\): all the extra coefficients are 0. \(H_A\): at least one is not 0.

\[F = \frac{(SSE_{\text{reduced}} - SSE_{\text{full}})\,/\,(\text{number of extra coefficients})}{SSE_{\text{full}}\,/\,df_{\text{full}}}\]

  • \(F\) near 1: the extra terms do nothing. A large \(F\) (small p-value) is evidence at least one extra term helps.
  • You should be able to read the output of anova(reduced, full) |> tidy()
  • For one extra coefficient, \(F = T^2\) and the p-values from an F-test and a T-test agree.
  • Use the F-test to test an interaction (anova(add_fit, int_fit)) or a categorical predictor with several indicators at once.
  • Multiple testing: many tests inflate false positives. State the primary hypothesis in advance. “Not significant” is not “no effect.”

Lecture 21: confidence intervals

\[\hat\beta_j \pm t^* \times SE_{\hat\beta_j}, \qquad t^* \text{ from a } t \text{ distribution with } n - p \text{ df}\]

  • The exam gives you \(t^*\). Width \(= 2\,t^*\,SE\). Narrower with more data, less noise, or a lower confidence level.
  • Correct interpretation: “We are 95% confident that as [x] increases by 1 [unit], [y] changes by [lower] to [upper] [units], on average (holding the other predictors constant).” It is about the method: 95% of intervals built this way capture the true parameter.
  • Wrong: “There is a 95% chance the true slope is in this interval” (the parameter is fixed, the interval is random).
  • A CI shows significance (does it contain 0?) and size (compare to a practical threshold, like a break-even).
  • Confidence interval (for the mean response at given \(x\)) vs. prediction interval (for a new individual response). The prediction interval is always wider. As \(n\) grows the CI shrinks toward zero width, but the PI cannot get narrower than about \(\pm 2\hat\sigma_\epsilon\).
  • R output: tidy(conf.int = TRUE) adds conf.low, conf.high.
  • R output: predict(..., interval = ...) gives fit, lwr, upr.

Lecture 22: conditions (LINE)

Condition How to check What a violation looks like
Linearity Residuals vs. fitted, and vs. each predictor Curvature or a pattern
Independence Study design, depends on the type of dependence Runs, clusters, repeated units
Normality Histogram and QQ plot of residuals QQ bow (skew), flared ends (heavy tails)
Equal variance Residuals vs. fitted Fan or funnel shape
  • Check conditions on residuals, because they remove the effects of the other predictors.
  • Worry most about Independence and Linearity, moderately about Equal variance, least about Normality (large \(n\) helps, but it matters more for small \(n\) and for prediction intervals).
  • When Equal variance fails, estimates are fine but standard errors are wrong.
  • Be able to look at a residual plot or QQ plot, name the condition, say whether it holds, and say what you’d do.

Lecture 23: multicollinearity

  • Multicollinearity: a predictor is strongly related to a combination of the other predictors.
  • Symptoms: inflated standard errors, unstable estimates, coefficients that change sharply or flip sign when a predictor is added, “holding the others constant” describes days that don’t exist.
  • Not harmed: predictions and \(R^2\) (the overall fit).

\[VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}, \qquad SE \text{ is inflated by a factor of } \sqrt{VIF_j}\]

  • Concern above \(VIF \approx 5\), serious above \(\approx 10\). With two predictors both VIFs equal \(1/(1-r^2)\).
  • Detect with cor(), scatterplots, and vif(). Interactions and squared terms inflate VIF by design.
  • Remedies: drop or combine predictors, or keep both and test them jointly with an F-test. Never drop a predictor your research question needs. Randomization reduces multicollinearity.

R output you might see

R output

  • dplyr code and tables of group means: what rows and columns remain, and how to turn group means into a regression equation.
  • Scatterplots and jitter plots with fitted lines or group means: residual signs, how a model is represented.
  • tidy() for an MLR: estimate, std.error, statistic, p.value (and conf.low, conf.high). Write the equation, predict, interpret each coefficient, compute \(T\) by hand, and compute a CI given \(t^*\).
  • glance(): r.squared, sigma (\(\hat\sigma_\epsilon\)), p.value. Compare models by \(R^2\) and \(\hat\sigma_\epsilon\).
  • supernova() / ANOVA table: SSM, SSE, SST, PRE; fill in a missing entry.
  • anova(reduced, full): read \(F\) and its p-value, find the missing entry, decide whether the extra terms help.
  • predict() with interval =: read fit, lwr, upr and say whether it is a confidence or prediction interval.
  • Residual plots, QQ plots, residuals vs. time: check LINE.
  • vif() or cor() output: diagnose multicollinearity.
  • A histogram of shuffled statistics with a red line: estimate the p-value.
